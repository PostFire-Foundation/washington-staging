Federal disaster declaration: yes, but not the one for individual assistance

On August 3, the President approved a federal emergency declaration for Washington (EM-3650). It covers six counties and three tribal nations, Spokane among them. Every member of the state's congressional delegation, both parties, had signed a letter asking for it to be approved quickly. That aid matters — and it's not yet the declaration that opens help for your household.

The emergency declaration pays for the response itself — crews, equipment, evacuation, coordination. That money goes to the agencies doing the work to keep people safe. There's no application open to individuals yet. If you've seen a headline since August 3 saying federal assistance was approved for Washington, this is what it's describing.

The declaration that opens assistance to individual households is a major disaster declaration. On August 4, the Governor's office said it is preparing that request. If and when it comes, we'll walk you through how to submit, break down why certain questions are asked the way they are, and help you get your application on its way. Don't forget to sign up for PostFire updates.

Until then, the support that already exists: your insurance, the Red Cross and 211, and state and local programs. Those don't wait on Washington, D.C.

The basics

You are not expected to know how to do this, and you do not have to do everything at once. We've broken out your immediate steps and we will follow up with what is next. The best thing you can do for yourself and your loved ones is get somewhere stable and safe, eat, and focus on getting some sleep tonight.

1

Stabilize housing first

Find a place you can stay two weeks or more, if you can. Rents rise immediately after a fire, and needing to move multiple times in a week can cost you precious energy your nervous system needs.

Save every receipt. If you have insurance, these costs can be reimbursed through a specific provision in your policy (we'll explain more below).

2

Follow official information only

Spokane County Emergency Management is where evacuation levels, re-entry, and assistance center locations get announced first. Sign up for Alert Spokane so those alerts reach your phone.

If you don't already have the Watch Duty app, download it to your phone now — real-time fire updates and evacuation orders for this area, and it's free.

To find out where your own address stands, use the county's evacuation lookup map. You type in your address and it tells you your current level. It's the same tool the emergency officials are using.

If your address comes back Level 3, don't go back yet. The fires are still burning and the lines are still moving.

A Disaster Assistance Center (DAC) is one building where the agencies come to you — IDs, titles, vital records, one trip. One is open now at Shadle Park High School; the address and hours are in the resources below. Leave your email and we will send you updates.

Is this page official? No — and that's exactly why we point you to what is.

3

Open your insurance claim now — and ask in writing

If you know your home is gone, or if your home is uninhabitable, call your insurer and open the claim. Ask for an advance on temporary housing money — on the phone, the words are “Additional Living Expenses” or “Loss of Use.” Ask in writing, and keep a copy of what you sent.

That coverage has a limit — sometimes a dollar amount, sometimes a number of months, sometimes both — and the clock on it started the day of the fire, not the day you call. Ask what your limit is and get the answer in writing, because it is the number that should decide whether you take a hotel or sign a lease.

If you can't pay your insurance premium right now, you have time. Washington's Insurance Commissioner has ordered every property and auto insurer in the state to give you a 45-day grace period, waive late fees, and not cancel your policy for nonpayment. It runs through September 30 and covers any zip code where homes burned or evacuation orders went out, plus the zip codes next to them.

You don't have to apply for this — it's on the insurer, not on you. If one tells you otherwise, call the Insurance Commissioner's office at 1-800-562-6900.

United Policyholders is a nonprofit that helps survivors navigate through the insurance process, for free. Watch out for anyone who wants a cut

4

Go ahead and buy what you need

If you need basics, go ahead and buy them. Keep your receipts for everything.

And if there are items that would make you feel most like you can establish any kind of routine, or feel like yourself, get them if you can.

5

Spot the scams

After every fire, the scammers arrive before the insurance checks do. These are the ways they come into a community:

Contractors going door to door

They show up in burn zones within days, often unlicensed and often from out of state, wanting a large cash deposit and a signature today. Verify anyone before you sign with Washington Labor & Industries' free contractor lookup (lni.wa.gov).

People posing as FEMA or government workers

Real disaster workers never charge you a fee, and never need your bank account or Social Security number to "process" your assistance. They carry photo ID, and you are allowed to ask to see it.

Rental listings that don't exist

Displaced families are the target: a good price, a landlord who is conveniently out of town, and a deposit wired before anyone sees the place. Don't send money for a home you haven't walked through.

Claim "helpers" who want a percentage

Some public adjusters are licensed and legitimate. Many who work a fresh burn zone are neither, and they want a cut of your settlement. Check the license with the Insurance Commissioner before anyone touches your claim — and remember United Policyholders does this for free.

Charities and fundraisers that aren't real

Urgent links, copied photos, and names built to sound almost like a real organization. Give only through organizations and people you can verify.

Debris and tree removal taken in cash

If anyone asks for money up front to do their work, be wary. You have time to figure out debris removal.

Free inspections that find expensive problems

Water testing, mold, air quality — a free look that turns into an urgent, costly finding and a contract to sign right now. Get a second opinion from someone who isn't selling the fix.

If you feel uncomfortable with pressure that someone offering help is putting you under, trust your gut. Real help doesn't rush you.

To report one, file with the Washington Attorney General.

If you're starting a GoFundMe

Keep the description general — this protects your other aid

Money raised for a specific purpose can create a “duplication of benefits” problem: if a fundraiser says it's for the same thing FEMA or your insurance is paying for, it can affect what you're eligible for.

Wording that works: “Funds to help bridge the gap while I recover.”

Wording to avoid: “to rebuild the house,” “to cover rent or hotel costs,” “to replace everything lost,” “to fix the property.”

Let someone else set it up

Someone who loves you can create the fundraiser for you — you don't have to write your own story while you're in the middle of it. Ask them to add you as a beneficiary so the money comes straight to you.

Before anyone clears the ash

This isn't something you have to handle today. It matters the moment someone offers to start clearing your lot — and knowing it now is what keeps that conversation from going sideways.

The debris has to be tested for asbestos first

Under Washington rules, what's left of a burned structure counts as demolition, and demolition means an asbestos survey before the debris is removed. Older homes are the usual reason — it was used in siding, roofing, floor tile, and pipe insulation, and fire doesn't destroy it.

How it actually works

An AHERA-certified building inspector samples the ash across your lot in a grid and sends it to a lab. If it comes back clean, you or your contractor file a Notice of Intent with Spokane Clean Air — the filing fee is waived — and that survey report is what lets the debris be taken as non-asbestos. If it comes back positive, the area gets taped off until a certified abatement company removes it.

That Notice of Intent number is also what the County needs before it will issue a demolition permit, so the survey isn't a detour around the permit — it's the way to it.

Ask them, not the person in your driveway

Spokane Regional Clean Air Agency wrote this guidance for fire-damaged homes and they answer the phone at (509) 477-4727, Monday through Friday, 8:00 to 4:30. If a contractor tells you the survey isn't necessary, that call settles it.

If someone wants to start hauling before the survey is done, that is your signal to slow down — and getting more than one quote for abatement is normal, not rude.

Nobody can make you sign today.

If your home was destroyed or badly damaged

Tzu Chi USA has opened emergency financial assistance to Washington wildfire families. Renters and homeowners both. It has to have been your primary residence, it has to have been destroyed or badly damaged, and it's one application per household.

If something about your situation doesn't fit the form, email relief-nc@tzuchi.us and ask before you decide you don't qualify.

Emergency shelter

The evacuation shelter is at the Spokane Convention Center, 334 W Spokane Falls Blvd, Spokane. Parking is free, and pets are welcome.

If you evacuated from south Stevens County, the Red Cross has a second shelter at Jenkins High School in Chewelah.

To check what's open before you drive, call the Red Cross at (509) 783-6195. The shelter at Spokane Falls Community College closed on August 1 — everyone there moved to the Convention Center.

Shelters move — this is current as of August 3. Check with Spokane County Emergency Management before you go.

Animals

The Barking Lot is accepting pets from people evacuating — 15825 E Trent Ave, Spokane Valley. Call (509) 564-6066 to check availability before you drive.

For large animals, the Spokane County Fairgrounds is open — use the red gate off Broadway. The RV grounds there are open too, for people who evacuated with animals.

SpokAnimal is boarding displaced pets for free at 710 N. Napa St.

If you can't care for your animal right now, SpokAnimal's Guardian Angel Program is who to call — (509) 534-8133. Asking for help with your pet is not the same as giving them up.

If your pet is missing, check with SpokAnimal, SCRAPS, and the vet clinics near where you were. Your neighborhood Facebook and Nextdoor groups are active for this too, and they're working.

Free pet food and supplies are at the Garland Theater & Bon Bon, 924 W. Garland Ave.

Air quality right now

The AirNow Fire and Smoke Map shows current smoke and air quality wherever you are — useful whether you evacuated or stayed. Spokane Regional Clean Air Agency issues the local alerts.

Smoke reaches far past the burn area. If the air is bad where you're staying, keep windows closed, run a filter if you have one, and keep kids and anyone with asthma or heart conditions indoors.
